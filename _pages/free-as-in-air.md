# Free as in Air

On the morning of the announcement the valley beyond the glass sat under its usual brown-gold haze. The press filled the fourth row back, storing their spare tanks under their seats on sitting down. The stage itself appeared to sit inside a bubble with no visible boundary; a conspicuous expense. At ten past ten the lights dropped. The wall behind the podium went white, then resolved into a single lowercase word in the company typeface.

*breathe.*

Applause from the first three rows, which were employees. Aera CEO Delia Vance walked out in the black t-shirt and blue sneakers that had become, over eleven years of company events, something like a uniform. She waited for the applause to end, and a beat longer.

"Good morning. It's wonderful to see everyone, and I want to say up front that I know how many liters it takes to get out to the valley. Thank you for coming."

Slide: a child on a rooftop, a small clear mask over her nose and mouth, looking up.

"Sixteen years ago, when Rafi and I started this company in a garage that, honestly, did not have great ventilation, we had a mission. We wrote it on the wall. Some of you have seen the photo. It said: *Make air for everyone.* And I think we've made real progress on that mission. We've brought the cost per liter down by ninety-four percent. We've delivered over two hundred trillion breaths." Applause. "Two hundred trillion. That's a lot of breaths. Aera Basic has put a clean, reliable, affordable supply within reach of more than a billion people. I'm incredibly proud of that."

Slide: the garage wall, sepia-toned.

"But I'm going to be honest with you. We didn't start Aera to make air affordable for _most_. We started Aera to make air for _everyone_. And for most of those sixteen years, that wasn't possible. The physics weren't there. The economics weren't there. We could bring the price down, and we did. But every liter still cost something to generate, to filter, to compress, to deliver. So everyone paid. Some people paid a lot. Some people paid with their health, or their kids' health. Everyone paid.

"Today, that changes."

Slide: *Aera Free.*

"This morning at six a.m. Pacific, we began releasing air. Not into canisters. Not into pipes. Into the atmosphere. The thing that's been sitting on top of all of us, mostly useless, our whole lives. As of about four hours ago, the ambient air across the greater valley region is breathable. No meter. No mask. No account." Then, because the room had gone completely still: "You can take your rebreathers off. Really. You've been able to since you arrived."

Nobody moved.

Vance smiled. "Rafi's going to walk you through the Generation Nine process, and then we'll take questions. But I want to be clear about what we're announcing, because I know how it sounds. This isn't a marketing stunt. We figured out how to make air at a marginal cost we can't measure. We tried to measure it. It rounds to zero. So we're giving it away. That's the product. It's air, and it's free."

---

Rafi Mendes, the CTO, took twelve minutes and used the word *stoichiometry* several times. The gist was that Gen Nine ran on ambient inputs and produced ambient outputs, and the difference between the two was that the latter could be breathed. A map of the valley turned from grey to pale blue in an expanding dot. Rollout to the coast by Friday. National by end of quarter. "Global," Mendes said, "is a shipping problem, not a physics problem, and we're good at shipping."

The house lights came up halfway. Vance sat on a stool at the edge of the stage, and the hands went up.

The financial wires went first. "Delia, congratulations. Can you walk us through the business model? Is Free going to be ad-supported? Is there a data component? What's the path to monetization?"

"Great question. There isn't one. It's free."

"Free as in..."

"As in you breathe it and no one sends you a bill."

"Sure, but the licensing. Is Aera retaining rights to the air once it's been inhaled? Exhaled? Can a third party capture and resell?"

"It's air. Once it's out, it's out. You can do whatever you want with it. Breathe it. Sell it, if you can find a buyer."

The trade weekly for the canister industry, standing. "Forty million people work in atmospheric services worldwide. Fill technicians. Meter readers. Filtration engineers. Mask fitters. Those are my readers. What's your message to them?"

"That they can breathe, for free, starting this morning."

"That doesn't really answer my question."

"I think it might be most of the answer. But I'll say more. Some of those jobs are going away. I won't pretend otherwise. And some of them only ever existed because air was expensive. Nobody grows up wanting to read meters." A murmur. "We've had some of the best engineers in the world working for us, and what we've had them doing is squeezing air into cans. I'd like to see what they do next."

"So you're comfortable with mass unemployment."

"I'm comfortable with people not needing to pay to breathe. I think the other part sorts itself out, and if it doesn't, I'd rather sort it out in a world where air is free than one where it isn't."

An environmental desk, second row. "The Gen Nine process. What's the footprint? You're releasing air at scale, continuously, into the atmosphere. Have you modeled the effects?"

Mendes leaned into his mic. "We published the model along with the release this morning. But the net effect on the atmosphere is that there's breathable air in it."

"There's induced demand to consider. When something is free, people consume more than they need. People will breathe more."

"More than they need?"

"More than they would otherwise."

"Than they would if it cost money," Mendes said. "Yes. Probably. We're hoping so."

A business columnist went next. "Isn't this just a play for total market control? You undercut every competitor to zero. They go under. And then Aera is the only supplier of air on the planet."

Vance shook her head. "We don't control it. We can't. That's what *release* means. If we switched off every generator tonight, what's already out there stays out there. It's in the sky. We spent sixteen years digging a moat, and this morning we filled it in."

"So you admit you're deliberately destroying the industry."

A pause. "Yes. I suppose I do."

Several people wrote that down.

A filtration trade reporter. "How do you guarantee purity? With canisters there's a chain of custody. Batch numbers. Ambient air could contain anything."

"It contains what air contains."

"You can't guarantee that."

"No," Vance said. "Nobody ever could. We just used to charge for it."

The public safety desk at one of the wires. "What about bad actors? Anyone can breathe now. Criminals. Extremists. People with air-debt judgments against them. There's no cutoff."

"That's right."

"And you're comfortable with that."

"I'm comfortable with people breathing, yes."

"Even people who haven't paid."

"Especially those people."

One of the long-form magazines. "There's a deeper question here, and I don't think it's been engaged with. Scarcity has shaped us. Breath discipline. The morning ration. Saving for a daughter's wedding mask. Teaching a child to hold his breath on the stairs. There's an entire literature. There's a way of being human that's organized around the cost of air. Are you at all concerned about what's lost when you make that meaningless? When air is just..." He gestures.

"I'm concerned about a great many things," Vance said. "That isn't one of them."

"That seems glib."

"It seems glib to me too. I've tried to find the version of that concern that I share and I can't. I'd trade every poem about the morning ration for one kid who no longer needs to."

Regulatory affairs, from one of the dailies. "Has the Atmospheric Commission signed off? Did you file?"

"We notified them at five forty-five this morning."

"Notified?"

"There isn't a form for it. We checked."

"Don't you think that should give you pause? Isn't this all moving too fast? Shouldn't there be a pause on until the implications are understood?"

"A pause on which part?"

"On the release."

"The release is done. It's in the air. That's where it is now." Vance turned her palms up. "I understand the instinct. I do. But I don't know how to un-release air, and I'm not sure I'd want to learn."

By this point the room had settled into something that was not quite hostility. It was closer to the collective displeasure of professionals who have flown across the country to cover an announcement and discovered that the announcement is *air is free now*, and that there is no angle on that, and who have concluded that the angle must therefore be hidden, and that it is their job to find it.

A student reporter in the sixth row, who had won a competition to be there, put his hand up and waited to be called on.

"Can I really take this off?"

Vance nodded at him. "Yes."

He reached up and unclipped his rebreather. The seal broke with the small wet pop they all make. He held it in his lap and breathed, and his chest went up and down, and nothing happened. He breathed again. He looked around at the room, then at Vance, and something in his face relaxed.

"It's nice," he said. "It's air."

Somewhere in the fourth row a wire reporter muttered, "Well, that's exactly the kind of stunt," and did not finish the sentence, and did not take off his mask.

Vance stood up off the stool. "I think that's time. Thank you, all of you. There's one more thing."

The screen behind her went white again.

"Aera Free is Aera Free. It is free, and it stays free. No caps, no ads, no account, no expiry. I want to be completely clear on that."

Slide: three columns resolved on the wall.

"But we also know that not everyone breathes the same way. Some of you have needs that ambient can't meet. So today we're also introducing Aera Plus, Aera Pro, and Aera Ultra.

"Plus is four ninety-nine a month. Twenty-two percent oxygen, so about a point up from ambient. Pollen and particulate filtering at the source. Family plan, up to six breathers.

"Pro is for people who breathe for a living. Athletes. Vocalists. Anyone in emergency services. Certified batch purity, our Alpine and Marine profiles, and priority supply in Aera-partnered venues. Twenty-nine ninety-nine.

"And Ultra." She let it hang, with a smile. "Ultra is what the young reporter and I are breathing right now. We don't publish pricing on Ultra. Talk to sales."

There was a silence of perhaps a second and a half.

Every hand in the room went up.
